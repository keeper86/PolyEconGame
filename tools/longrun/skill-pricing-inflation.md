# Pricing & inflation — data-proven findings

Three findings, all measured in the 8B-population long runs rather than inferred from code:

**1. Inflation is cost-push from the theoretical cost factor, not the tâtonnement.** With `THEORETICAL_PRODUCTION_COST_FACTOR = 1.3`, every supply-chain stage applies a ~1.3–1.7× markup that compounds down the chain; setting it to 1.0 cut the manufactured price level from ~1,236 to ~160 at y50 (~8×) at an identical bankruptcy rate (97 vs 96 by y60) and identical facility condition (0.996). The construction sector proves it's cost-push rather than a demand shock: its margin is flat (~0.5–0.6) and price-over-cost flat (~1.2–1.5) across the whole run, even while its supply often exceeds demand 17×.

**2. The sell-through target knob moves volatility, not the price level.** Sell 1.1 produced 86 starvation months over 30 years, sell 1.2 produced 0 — yet their price levels were comparable; the sell target only decides whether a shortage tips into starvation, while the price level is set by the cost markup. The buy-side double-counting (`refillRate`) was the second-biggest inflation engine (~12×: plMan 15,335 → 1,236 at y50), but it also acted as an over-bidding buffer that kept input buffers full, so removing it traded lower prices for more frequent starvation events. Lowering the cost-spring buffer (1.5 → 1.2) was counterproductive.

**3. The recurring starvation is an expansion failure, not a bankruptcy cascade.** In the food chain, facility maxScale froze (food processor 754k, grocery 1.66M) while population grew +64%, so the food processor pinned at `scaleFrac = 1.00` and demand finally exceeded the frozen ceiling. It stayed profitable throughout (margins 0.37–0.86, profits in the billions) and `expansionBlockedByProfit` stayed ~0–4 — the autoscale controller simply never expanded capacity as the slack was consumed. Wages lag food prices (167× vs 491×), so real wages fall and households sit at `wealthToFoodPrice ≈ 0.2`, tipping into starvation at each food-chain pinch.
